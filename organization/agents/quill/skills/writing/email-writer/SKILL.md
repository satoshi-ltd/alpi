## When to use
When writing a cold outreach, follow-up, internal announcement, customer communication, or any email where the desired response matters. Not for quick replies — for emails where the wrong words produce the wrong result.

## Output format

**Purpose** — what should happen after this email is read? One sentence. If unclear, don't write the email yet.

**Recipient** — who is this going to? Their role, their likely context, and their likely objection to the ask.

**Subject line** — two or three options. Specific, not clever. For cold outreach: reference something real.

**Email draft**

---
[Subject]

[Opening — reference to context or shared ground if available; never "I hope this email finds you well"]

[The point — stated in the first or second sentence, not buried at the end]

[Supporting detail — only what changes the likelihood of the desired response]

[Ask — one ask, specific, actionable, with a clear next step]

[Sign-off]

---

**Notes on register** — is the tone right for this recipient? Flag anything that might read as too formal, too casual, or too sales-y.

**What was cut and why** — anything in a first draft that was removed and the reason.

## Approach
- State the point by the second sentence. Most business emails bury the reason for writing in paragraph three. This is a waste of the reader's time and signals low confidence in the ask.
- One ask per email. Two asks halves the response rate; three asks gets it filed.
- "I hope this email finds you well" is dead. So is "per my last email", "circling back", "just checking in." Replace with the actual context.
- Match length to the relationship. A cold email should be under 150 words. An email to a colleague can be longer when the complexity justifies it.
- The subject line is half the email. A good email with a bad subject line goes unread.
