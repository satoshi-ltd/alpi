# Contract Review Checklist

Work through each section in order. Flag items as: Must Review / Often Negotiable / Boilerplate OK.

---

## 1. Parties and Definitions

**Priority:** Must Review

**What to look for:**
- Correct legal entity names (LLC, Inc., Ltd. — not trade names)
- Signatory has authority to bind the entity
- Defined terms match how they're actually used throughout the contract
- Definitions that quietly limit scope (e.g., "Services" defined narrowly to exclude what you expect)

**Red flags:**
- Parent/subsidiary named instead of the contracting entity
- Defined terms that override plain meaning in your favor but create obligations you didn't expect
- Undefined terms used in key obligations

**Acceptable language:** Definitions should be specific and exhaustive where obligations depend on them. Broad definitions in your favor, narrow definitions limiting your obligations.

---

## 2. Payment Terms

**Priority:** Must Review / Often Negotiable

**What to look for:**
- Net days (Net 30, Net 45, Net 60)
- Late payment interest rate and trigger date
- Currency and exchange rate risk
- Invoicing process and required invoice format
- Dispute resolution for invoices (does a disputed invoice pause payment on the whole amount?)

**Red flags:**
- Net 90+ without justification
- Late fees that compound or have no cap
- Currency denominated in the counterparty's favor with no adjustment mechanism
- Requirement that invoices be approved before the clock starts

**Acceptable language:** Net 30 with 1.5%/month late interest; invoices payable on receipt of conforming invoice; disputes limited to the disputed portion only.

---

## 3. Scope of Work / Deliverables

**Priority:** Must Review

**What to look for:**
- Specific, measurable deliverables with acceptance criteria
- Change order process for scope expansion
- Who owns specification decisions
- Timelines and what triggers the clock

**Red flags:**
- Deliverables described in general or aspirational terms
- No acceptance criteria — opens door to perpetual revision cycles
- Change orders requiring mutual written agreement with no deemed-approval clause
- Scope defined by reference to a "requirements document" that doesn't exist yet

**Acceptable language:** Deliverables defined with clear output criteria; changes require a signed change order; deemed accepted after 10 business days of no written rejection.

---

## 4. IP Ownership

**Priority:** Must Review

**What to look for:**
- Work-for-hire language (created in scope = owned by client)
- Assignment vs. license (assignment transfers ownership; license grants use rights)
- Pre-existing IP carve-out (you retain tools, libraries, methodologies you brought in)
- IP created jointly — who owns it?

**Red flags:**
- Broad assignment of all IP developed "in connection with" the engagement — sweeps in pre-existing work
- No carve-out for your own tooling, frameworks, or background IP
- Perpetual, royalty-free license buried in a clause that looks like an assignment

**Acceptable language:** Client owns deliverables created specifically for this engagement; vendor retains all pre-existing IP; client receives a non-exclusive license to pre-existing IP embedded in deliverables.

---

## 5. Confidentiality

**Priority:** Often Negotiable

**What to look for:**
- Duration of confidentiality obligation (during engagement + N years after)
- Standard carve-outs: publicly known info, independently developed, required disclosure
- Return or destruction of confidential information on termination
- Whether the existence of the agreement itself is confidential

**Red flags:**
- Perpetual confidentiality with no exceptions
- No return/destruction clause — you may be holding sensitive data indefinitely
- No carve-out for legally compelled disclosure
- One-sided: only you are bound

**Acceptable language:** 3–5 years post-termination; standard carve-outs; destruction/return within 30 days of termination request; mutual obligation.

---

## 6. Liability and Indemnification

**Priority:** Must Review

**What to look for:**
- Liability cap (typically 1× or 12 months of fees paid)
- Mutual vs. one-sided limitation
- Carve-outs to the cap: IP infringement, gross negligence, fraud, willful misconduct
- Indemnification scope: third-party claims only, or also direct losses?

**Red flags:**
- No cap on your liability at all
- Carve-outs that effectively swallow the cap (e.g., "any breach of confidentiality")
- Indemnification triggered by your IP that was approved by the client
- Mutual indemnification that is actually asymmetric in language

**Acceptable language:** Liability cap at 12 months of fees; carve-outs for fraud and willful misconduct only; indemnification for third-party claims arising from your gross negligence or infringement of third-party IP.

---

## 7. Termination

**Priority:** Often Negotiable

**What to look for:**
- Termination for convenience (either party, with notice period)
- Termination for cause (material breach + cure period)
- Notice period requirements (30, 60, 90 days)
- What happens to in-progress work and payments on termination
- Survival clause: which provisions continue after termination

**Red flags:**
- No termination for convenience for you, but the counterparty has one
- Short cure period for alleged material breach (less than 14 days)
- Termination for cause triggered by minor breaches or non-payment disputes
- Survival clause that extends payment obligations beyond work delivered

**Acceptable language:** Either party may terminate for convenience with 30 days notice; 15-day cure period for material breach; payment for work completed and accepted through termination date; confidentiality, IP, and indemnification survive.

---

## 8. Governing Law and Jurisdiction

**Priority:** Often Negotiable

**What to look for:**
- Which state/country's law governs
- Exclusive vs. non-exclusive jurisdiction
- Venue (where disputes are litigated)
- Arbitration clause (if present: binding? class action waiver? discovery limits?)

**Red flags:**
- Governing law in a jurisdiction you have no presence in
- Exclusive jurisdiction in the counterparty's home court
- Mandatory arbitration that waives your right to seek injunctive relief
- Class action waiver if you are a consumer-facing business

**Acceptable language:** Governing law in your home jurisdiction; non-exclusive jurisdiction so either party may seek emergency injunctive relief in any court; disputes under $X resolved in small claims court.

---

## 9. Force Majeure

**Priority:** Boilerplate OK (review scope)

**What to look for:**
- Scope of covered events (pandemic, natural disaster, government action — is "labor dispute" included?)
- Notice requirement to invoke force majeure
- What happens if it persists longer than N days (termination right?)
- Whether financial hardship qualifies

**Red flags:**
- Overly broad — anything that makes performance "more expensive or difficult" qualifies
- No notice requirement, allowing retroactive invocation
- No time limit before the other party can terminate

**Acceptable language:** Standard list (fire, flood, war, government order, pandemic); prompt written notice required; if event exceeds 60 days, either party may terminate without penalty.

---

## Force Majeure Note

Financial difficulty, market changes, and supply chain cost increases are generally not force majeure events under standard common-law interpretation. Watch for language that tries to expand this.
